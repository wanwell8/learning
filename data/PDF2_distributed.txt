I NTRODUCTION S THE development of large scale photovoltaic (PV\ plants in load centers becomes saturated, more and more PV power are integrated into distribution systems as distributed energy resources (DERs\ To deal with poten- tial voltage violations induced by continuously increasing PV penetration, small-size PV inverters are allowed to actively participate in system voltage regulation through providing Var compensation by the IEEE 1547 standard A. Literature Review To achieve efcient Volt-Var control, distributed PV invert- ers tend to operate in a coordinated way. Such multi-inverter based voltage control tasks were conventionally modelled as centralized optimization problems assuming the availability of global information . Furthermore, to achieve better scal- ability as well as improve robustness to single point failures, centralized optimal voltage control problems were decom- posed into several sub-problems, which could be solved in a distributed way with only local and neighbor information . It is worth noting, both centralized and distributed volt- age control methods mentioned above had to be iteratively solved before the converged solution can be applied on dis- tributed PV inverters. However, considering the volatile nature of PV power, control commands gured out by above meth- ods may lag behind the variation of PV power in real time To facilitate online implementation, for example in a gradient-projection based Volt-Var control scheme was designed for coordinated voltage regulation. With this method, distributed inverters could rapidly respond to system volt- age mismatch without requiring any real time communication. , a dual-ascent algorithm was adopted for distributed online voltage control. Particularly, the control command of PV inverters would be immediately carried out in each iteration step, with guaranteed optimality and convergence. Other optimization based online voltage control schemes can also be seen in ,etc. Except for optimization based schemes, consensus algo- rithms are also widely used to coordinate distributed invert- ers for voltage control. For example, reactive power output was reallocated among PV inverters by consensus algorithms to promote an effective and fair use of the over- all Var compensation capacity. Furthermore, consensus based schemes were extended to be applied in cases of three-phase unbalanced distribution systems such as in . While, early researches often relied on static consensus for coordi- nation, where algorithms needed to be iteratively solved in a closed form. To improve real-time control performance, dynamic consensus algorithms were designed to respond to time-varying signals in each iteration step . For exam- ple, a dynamic consensus algorithm was adopted in track the average of time-varying Var outputs of distributed PV inverters. Similarly in , inverters were coordinated by a dynamic average consensus for Var compensation shar- ing. Most of these researches assumed perfect communication environment. The performance of a distributed control algorithm heav- ily depends on the communication network it relies on. For small-size PV inverters, they can easily gain access to wire- less communication networks with low price, but at the same time, they have to face the inevitable time delays and packet dropout. To deal with random communication delay, an asyn- chronous distributed control algorithm was proposed in for coordinated Volt-Var control. A freezing strategy was adopted in a distributed voltage control algorithm in counter random communication link failures. Synchronous and asynchronous voltage control methodologies were proposed and compared in , and a weighted autoregressive strategy was designed to improve convergence speed under random communication delays. Similarly, the impact of communica- tion delay on control performance was discussed in with simulation results, and an asynchronous approach was adopted in to accelerate convergence. In , communi- cation delay was added into the derived small-signal model of a microgrid, and on this basis delay-dependent criteria were then developed to detect the stability of the microgrid by trac- ing critical eigenvalues and cluster treatment of characteristic B.
